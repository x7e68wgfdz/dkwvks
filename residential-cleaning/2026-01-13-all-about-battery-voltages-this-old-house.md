Related How To Select Power Tool Batteries What’s Inside a Power Tool Battery?

To understand how power tool batteries work, let’s take a look inside. A typical battery contains individual cells and a circuit board that work together to power your tools.

Each lithium-ion cell is rated at 3.6 volts, but when fully charged, it actually holds about four volts. So in an 18-volt battery with five cells, the math adds up to 18 volts of usable power—but when the pack is fully charged and not under load, a voltmeter will read 20 volts. This is why you’ll sometimes see the same battery platform marketed as both “18V” and “20V Max.” Battery Cells

At the core of a power tool battery are individual cells resembling AA or C batteries. These cells are arranged in specific ways to achieve the voltage and capacity you need for your tools. The better these cells are organized, the more efficiently the battery delivers power. This, in turn, impacts the tool’s performance. Circuit Board

Modern lithium-ion batteries have an advanced circuit board that: Shows the battery charge level Manages power distribution

Helps the battery use its cells efficiently Prevents overheating

This sophisticated circuitry makes today’s batteries much smarter and safer than older models that had fewer protections and didn’t distribute power as efficiently. Understanding Battery Voltage

Volts roughly indicate how much power is packed into a tool’s battery, while amp-hours reflect how long a tool can deliver that power. Both are a function of how many cells are in a battery pack and how they’re wired together. How Voltage Is Determined

The way voltage is calculated has shifted along with battery technology. As noted in This Old House Magazine, older cordless tool batteries used cells rated at just 1.2 volts each—meaning a 12-volt pack required 10 cells wired together. Today’s lithium-ion cells, rated at 3.6 volts each, deliver the same 12 volts with only three cells, which is a major reason modern battery packs are so much lighter and more compact.

A battery’s voltage is determined by its cell count. Typically, each lithium-ion cell has a nominal voltage of 3.6 volts. For example:

An 18V battery has five 3.6V cells (3.6V x 5 = 18V)

A 12V battery has three 3.6V cells (3.6V x 3 = 10.8V)

Although these voltage designations are standard, they can often cause confusion among users. The 18V vs. 20V Debate

You may notice manufacturers labeling their batteries as either 18V or 20V. In many cases, these distinctions refer to the same battery capacity. Here’s why:

When fully charged, each 3.6V cell can reach up to 4V. That means an 18V battery (which has five cells) can go up to 20V when fully charged (4V x 5 = 20V). However, under load, the battery drops back to its nominal 18V rating. So, the difference between an 18V and 20V battery often comes down to marketing rather than a noticeable difference in power.

What Are Battery Amp-Hours and Why Do They Matter?

While voltage impacts performance, amp-hours (Ah) tell you how long a battery can last under load. What Do Amp-Hours Measure?

Amp-hours measure a battery’s ability to deliver current over time. A battery with a higher Ah rating can:

Deliver more runtime for power-hungry tools Handle intense power requirements Sustain voltage over extended periods

Essentially, the Ah rating helps you estimate how much work the battery can handle before it runs out.

Pro Tip: To get a more complete picture of a battery’s total energy, multiply its voltage by its amp-hour rating—the result is “watt hours.” As This Old House Magazine has explained, a 14.4-volt battery rated at 2.6Ah delivers over 37 watt hours, which could actually outperform a higher-voltage battery with a lower Ah rating. More watt hours means more work before you need a recharge. Matching Amp-Hours to Your Tools

Follow these guidelines for selecting a battery with the correct amp-hour rating for your tool:

Low-draw tools like drills can work well on batteries with lower Ah ratings.

Different tasks require different voltage levels:

12V systems are ideal for light, compact tools.

18V/20V systems are versatile enough for most home projects.
