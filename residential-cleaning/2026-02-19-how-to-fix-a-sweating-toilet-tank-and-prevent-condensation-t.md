# How To Fix a Sweating Toilet Tank and Prevent Condensation - This Old House

Plumbing expert Richard Trethewey explains the causes of a sweating toilet and how to fix one.

A sweating toilet tank can be more than just an annoyance—it can lead to water damage and mold growth if left unchecked. This common household problem occurs when warm, humid air condenses on the cold surface of the toilet tank, causing it to “sweat” and drip water onto the floor. In this article, we explore the causes of a sweating toilet tank and how to fix it. Richard Trethewey, This Old House’s plumbing expert, demonstrates a practical solution to this problem in the video. Causes of a Sweating Toilet Tank

Several factors can contribute to a sweating toilet tank: High humidity levels in the bathroom Cold water supply temperature Lack of proper ventilation

Frequent toilet use, which introduces more cold water into the tank

High humidity levels, often exacerbated by hot showers and baths, increase the amount of moisture in the air. When this moisture-laden air encounters the cold surface of the toilet tank, condensation is inevitable. Similarly, the temperature of the water supply plays a significant role. In regions where the groundwater is particularly cold, the likelihood of condensation increases. Proper ventilation is crucial. Without it, moist air lingers longer in the bathroom, promoting condensation. Potential Consequences

While a sweating toilet tank might seem like a minor inconvenience, it can lead to more serious issues if left unaddressed: Water damage to flooring Mold and mildew growth Structural damage to the subfloor Increased slip hazard in the bathroom

The water damage caused by a consistently sweating toilet tank can warp and rot wooden flooring. This not only affects the aesthetics of your bathroom but also its structural integrity. Mold and mildew thrive in moist environments, and a constantly dripping tank provides the perfect conditions for them to grow. This can pose health risks to you and your family. Water dripping onto the floor also increases the risk of slips and falls.

On an episode of Ask This Old House, homeowner Ajay discovered firsthand how damaging a sweating toilet can be. After noticing stains on his basement ceiling, he pulled down the panels expecting to find a plumbing leak—only to realize it was condensation from the sweating toilet tank above dripping down and ruining his ceiling. As Ajay explained to Richard Trethewey: “This place was leaking all this area… I figured it wasn’t any plumbing that was leaking, but it was the sweating. Condensation was dripping downstairs and it ruined my ceiling.”

Diagnosing the Problem With a Toilet Tank

Before attempting to fix a sweating toilet tank, confirm that condensation is indeed the issue. Sometimes, what appears to be a sweating tank could actually be a leak. Checking for Leaks To rule out leaks: Dry the outside of the tank thoroughly.

Place a few drops of food coloring in the tank water. Wait 30 minutes without flushing.

Check the floor and the outside of the tank for colored water.

If you see colored water, you likely have a leak that needs to be addressed differently than condensation. Leaks could stem from tank cracks or faulty plumbing. Addressing them might involve sealing or replacing parts. Assessing Humidity Levels

Use a hygrometer to measure the humidity levels in your bathroom. If the relative humidity is consistently above 60%, this could be contributing to the condensation problem.

Simple Sweating Toilet Tank Solutions To Try First

Before undertaking more complex fixes, try these simple solutions:

Improve bathroom ventilation by using an exhaust fan during and after showers.

Keep the bathroom door open when possible to allow air circulation.

Use a dehumidifier in the bathroom to reduce moisture levels.

Insulate exposed cold water pipes leading to the toilet.

Use an open window for extra ventilation.

Before trying any of these fixes, Richard Trethewey advises checking whether the toilet tank is constantly running. As he explains: “The first thing I always look at with something like this is, is the toilet tank running? Because if we brought new water in all the time, it would make any toilet tank condense.” A running toilet continuously draws in fresh cold water from the supply line, making condensation far worse—and no amount of ventilation will solve the problem until the running is fixed. Installing an Anti-Sweat Valve

For a more permanent solution, installing an anti-sweat valve can effectively prevent condensation. This valve mixes a small amount of hot water with the cold water supply to raise the temperature of the water in the toilet tank, reducing the likelihood of condensation. Pro Tip: When installing the anti-sweat valve, orientation matters. If they didn’t work, we might end up with hot water at a cold water faucet.” Also note that the valve is adjustable—on a really humid day you can increase the hot water mix, then turn it right off in winter when condensation isn’t an issue. Adjust anti-sweat valve settings as needed for seasonal temperature changes.

Keeping an eye on the bathroom’s condition through regular checks can prevent minor issues from escalating into significant problems. Seasonal adjustments ensure that methods such as the anti-sweat valve remain effective throughout the year.

When managing bathroom moisture levels seasonally, opt for air-conditioning instead of open windows to cool the house during warm months. Keeping indoor humidity levels below 50 percent is critical—that’s the threshold that thwarts the spread of mold and mildew, which can take hold around a chronically sweating toilet tank.

Pro Tip: Don’t overlook your bathroom’s exhaust fan during quarterly inspections. Listen for unusual sounds that signal worn bearings in the motor—a failing vent fan can’t pull moisture out of the room effectively, which directly contributes to toilet tank condensation. While you’re at it, check that insulation in adjacent crawl spaces or attic areas is dry; wet insulation near bathroom plumbing can compound humidity problems.
