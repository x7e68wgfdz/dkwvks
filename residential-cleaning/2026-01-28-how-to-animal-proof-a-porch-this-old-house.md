Understanding the Need for Animal-Proofing

Before diving into this project, we’ll list common animals that might be attracted to your porch and the potential risks they pose. Common Porch Invaders

Several types of animals may seek shelter under a porch, including the following: Feral cats Groundhogs Opossums Raccoons Skunks

When these animals are in search of a den or nest, they’re often drawn to porches because of their privacy and cover. Risks of Animal Intrusion

You may be tempted to allow an animal to take up residence under your porch, but it can lead to various problems, such as:

Aggressive encounters with pets or humans Health hazards from animal waste Structural damage to the porch Unpleasant odors

Additionally, these critters can carry diseases, such as rabies. Since they pose a significant health risk to both humans and pets, you have to address issues promptly and effectively.

The damage can escalate quickly once animals settle in. As one homeowner in Teaneck, New Jersey, discovered after squirrels targeted his Dutch Colonial’s deep eaves: “The squirrels would stand in the gutters and nibble through the fascia,” he recalls. “I had squirrels in my attic, squirrels in my living room, squirrels nesting, squirrels having babies.” What began as a minor nuisance forced him to launch a full-scale campaign against the invaders—a scenario that plays out in homes across the country as suburban development encroaches on wildlife habitat.

Step-by-Step Guide to Animal-Proofing Your Porch

Once you’ve gathered your tools, it’s time to get started. Follow the steps below to create a barrier that will stop animals from accessing the space under your porch. 1. Prepare the Area

By clearing the area around and underneath your porch, you’ll get a clearer understanding of what you need to do to prevent animals from entering the space.

Remove any debris or vegetation that might attract animals.

Inspect the porch for existing entry points or damage.

Measure the perimeter of the porch to determine how much hardware cloth you’ll need. 2. Dig a Trench

Animals can dig under shallow fencing, so creating a deep barrier will block them from burrowing. Below are the steps to create an effective obstruction.

Dig an 8- to 12-inch-deep trench alongside the porch perimeter.

Make the trench wide enough to accommodate the hardware cloth.

Remove any large rocks or roots that might interfere with the installation. 3. Prepare the Hardware Cloth

Hardware cloth creates an effective barrier by blocking access to the space, but you have to use it the right way. Here’s how:

Cut the hardware cloth slightly larger than the opening under the porch.

Use a two-by-four to bend a 6-inch-wide L-shaped flange into the hardware cloth. This L-shape will create an underground barrier to prevent animals from digging underneath. 4. Install the One-Way Door (Optional)

The steps outlined above will prevent animals from forming a new nest or den under your porch, but what if you suspect that one is already living there? A one-way door will allow them to leave but not reenter. In the video, Silva explains that creating a one-way door is a humane way to get rid of the animal since there isn’t a risk of it getting trapped inside. This is most effective when young animals aren’t present.

Additional Tips for Keeping Animals Away

There are a few other ways you can deter animals besides creating this barrier:

Add animal repellent around your property’s perimeter.

Remove any food sources by securing trash cans and cleaning up fallen fruit or birdseed.

Trim trees and shrubs away from the porch.

Use motion-activated lights or sprinklers to startle animals as they approach your porch. Enhanced Repellent Solutions

If you have persistent animal problems, there are stronger repellent solutions you can use to repel animals. Solutions include items such as scent-based and electronic deterrents. Scent-Based Repellents

Scent-based repellents use things that repulse animals, such as predator urine or strong unpleasant odors. You can apply these around your porch or other parts of your yard. Use safety gear when you’re putting these products down.

Pro Tip: Predator urine sprays can be especially effective for smaller animals like chipmunks, as well as deer and raccoons, but they need consistent reapplication. As noted in This Old House Magazine, “Be sure to follow the product directions and reapply as often as suggested, because if you don’t keep up the pressure the chipmunks won’t take you seriously.” Also test a small area first—spray a discreet section of your porch or patio surface to make sure the urine doesn’t stain it. Electronic Deterrents

Ultrasonic devices or motion-activated sprinklers are two examples of electronic deterrents. This technology can startle animals or discourage them from entering the area. Place these devices strategically, covering entry points and common paths wildlife uses. Maintaining Your Animal-Proof Barrier

Setting up a routine check will help you maintain your efforts to create an effective barrier because you’ll catch issues early and fix them before an animal can break through. Here are a few tips:

Check for new entry points that animals may have made over time.

Inspect the barrier periodically for signs of damage or wear.

Keep the area around the porch clear of debris and overgrowth.

Repair any breaches in the hardware cloth immediately.

Humane Considerations When Detering Animals

Although you’re trying to stop animals from building nests or dens under your porch, you must take humane steps so they’re not hurt as you deter them:

Avoid sealing entry points during breeding seasons when young animals may be present.

Consult with local wildlife experts if you’re unsure about the best approach for your situation.

If you think animals are living under your porch, use ways to help them exit the area safely before you seal the space.

Pro Tip: Pat Vissering, a senior wildlife biologist with the New York State Department of Conservation, cautions against trapping and relocating animals: “Trapping and releasing an animal often dooms it to starve in an unknown territory.” In many areas, transporting live wildlife is actually illegal, making humane exclusion methods—rather than removal—the preferred approach.

Allowing animals to exit safely and preventing their return is the most ethical solution.
