This article appeared in the Summer 2021 issue of This Old House Magazine.

With its deep seat, tall back, and wide armrests, there’s arguably no more restful spot to while away the day than an Adirondack chair. It’s little wonder these seats are a nearly ubiquitous feature on lawns nationwide in Summer.

While you can purchase Adirondack chairs, building your own allows for customization and can be a rewarding DIY project. This Old House general contractor Tom Silva, alongside host Kevin O’Connor, shows how to construct a durable Adirondack chair using pressure-treated lumber. Adirondack Chair Overview Peter Sucheski

As its name implies, the chair’s early-20th-century design originated in the Adirondack Mountains of New York. Its signature details­—the angle of the back and the slope, depth, and curve of the seat­—contribute to its unparalleled comfort. The version Tom chose to build, from a set of woodworking plans readily available online (Rockler.com), has a gently curved back and an arched top.

If you plan to use pressure-treated lumber, as recommended, it will typically cost between $15 and $25 per square foot, according to Angi. Steps for Building an Adirondack Chair Step 1: Make the Templates Doug Levy

Place the full-size cardboard templates from the plans on the MDF and cut out each shape with a jigsaw.

Sand the MDF edges smooth to the edge of the cardboard, and drill 11⁄64-inch pilot holes in the MDF following the marks on the cardboard.

Next, use a miter saw to cut each piece of lumber to length. Screw the templates to them.

Pro Tip: Tom Silva, This Old House general contractor, advises building a tapering jig for the back supports and slats: “It’s nothing but a scrap piece of plywood. I cut some slots in it so I can use this and angle it any way I want.” Since each back slat requires a different taper angle, this adjustable jig lets you make precise, repeatable cuts on the table saw without resetting a fence each time. Step 2: Cut the Curved Pieces Doug Levy

Using a band saw or jigsaw, cut alongside each cardboard/MDF template, taking care not to let the blade touch it.

To save time making pairs of curved armrests and support pieces, Tom used a rafter square (A) to align one edge of the MDF template (B) with the edges of the stock (C). Then he screwed all of them together so they wouldn’t slip as he cut the paired pieces on a band saw. Before unscrewing them, he did a final trim on all the edges with a flush-cutting bit, running its bearing along the template. Tip: Coating Pressure-Treated Wood

Treated woods often have such a high moisture content that it takes three to six months to accept a finish. For kiln-dried wood stamped KDAT or KD19, the wait is one or two months.

Tom used the Classic Adirondack Chair Template and Project Plan from Rockler to figure out how to make all the different cuts. The plan comes with cardboard templates for the different chair pieces and some project plans to use as a guide.
