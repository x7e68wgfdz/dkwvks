How Thermostatic Radiator Valves Work

[This Old House](https://this-old-house.northlist.shop/this-old-house/20260824.html)

Thermostatic radiator valves control heat output from individual radiators. By installing these, you can create zones within your heating system for strategic temperature control in different areas of your home. This is especially beneficial in larger homes or rooms that aren’t used frequently.

Traditional radiator valves don’t adjust for room temperature changes, which can lead to overheating and wasted energy. Thermostatic valves prevent this by regulating heat output more precisely. Thermostatic radiator valves contain a temperature-sensitive element that expands or contracts based on the room’s temperature. This adjusts the valve’s opening, regulating the flow of hot water into the radiator. As a result, the radiator’s heat output is adjusted to maintain the desired room temperature.

In addition to lowering your energy bills and improving your home’s comfort, thermostatic valves can reduce the strain on your boiler and other components of your heating system, potentially extending their lifespan. By using less energy to heat your home, they also lessen your carbon footprint.

Before starting the installation, turn off the heating system and allow the radiator to cool completely. Place drop cloths on the floor to protect the surrounding work area.

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey notes that thermostatic radiator valves effectively “make every single radiator its own zone.” As he explained during a This Old House project visit, if you set a valve to 70 degrees and the room reaches that temperature, “nothing comes through”—preventing overheating and eliminating the need to open windows for relief. Step-by-Step Installation Process

Follow the steps below to successfully install your thermostatic radiator valves. 1. Draining the Radiator

Begin by draining the water from the radiator. This step will prevent water damage and make the valve removal and installation easier.

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey advises on This Old House that draining the system is the essential first step: “The most important thing to do is to drain the heating system before you do this.” He also cautions that when tightening fittings, you need to find the right balance: “You wanna tighten it so that it doesn’t leak, but you don’t wanna over-tighten”—over-tightening can crack the bonnet or damage old radiator threads, creating an even bigger problem. 2. Removing the Old Valve

Use pipe wrenches to disconnect the old valve from the radiator. In the video, Trethewey demonstrates how to loosen and remove the valve body from the threaded pipe. 3. Removing the Spud

The spud is a threaded fitting that connects the valve to the radiator. Use a spud wrench to remove it, as shown in the video. The spud has small nubs that require this specialized tool. 4. Preparing the New Spud

Apply thread sealant to the first few threads of the new spud. Then, wrap single-strand wicking around the threads. This combination helps create a tight seal between the new threads and the old threads in the radiator.

Pro Tip: When preparing the new spud, a belt-and-suspenders approach to thread sealing works best. As demonstrated on This Old House, first apply pipe dope around the threads, then wrap single-strand wicking over them. This combination is especially important when threading new fittings into older radiator connections, where worn threads make achieving a watertight seal more challenging than with new-to-new connections. 5. Installing the New Spud

Use the spud wrench to install the new spud into the radiator, making sure it’s tightened securely to prevent leaks. 6. Preparing the Existing Pipe

Apply pipe dope and single-strand wicking to the threads of the existing pipe. This step helps create a secure connection between the pipe and the new valve. 7. Installing the New Valve

Carefully thread the new thermostatic radiator valve onto the existing pipe. Use a pipe wrench to tighten it securely. 8. Sealing the Connection

Apply pipe sealant to the face of the spud before connecting it to the new valve. This extra step gives the valve a watertight seal. 9. Tightening the Connections

Use pipe wrenches to tighten the connection between the spud and the new valve. Be careful not to overtighten, which could damage the components. 10. Installing the Sensor

For radiators with covers, you’ll need to install the temperature sensor. Here’s how:

Drill a small hole in the radiator cover. Feed the sensor wire through the hole.

Secure the sensor to the side of the radiator cover using zip screws. Connect the sensor wire to the valve. 11. Finishing Touches

Replace the radiator cover and check that the sensor wire isn’t kinked or damaged. Make any final adjustments to the valve’s position until it’s properly aligned. Related

Programming and Using Your New Thermostatic Radiator Valve

Once installed, your new thermostatic radiator valve will need to be programmed and adjusted for best performance. Setting the Desired Temperature

Most thermostatic radiator valves have a numbered dial corresponding to different temperature settings. Consult your valve’s manual to understand the temperature range and set it accurately. Adjusting for Different Times of Day

Some advanced thermostatic radiator valves let you program different temperatures throughout the day. This feature helps you save energy when rooms are unoccupied. Maintenance Tips

To keep your thermostatic radiator valves functioning properly, regularly check for leaks around the valve and connections. Clean the valve and sensor as needed to remove dust and debris. Test the valve’s responsiveness by adjusting the settings and monitoring room temperature. Troubleshooting Common Issues

Even with careful installation, your new thermostatic radiator valves may have some issues, including unresponsiveness to temperature changes, heating issues, and leaks. Here are some solutions to try.

Valve Not Responding to Temperature Changes

If the valve doesn’t seem to be adjusting to temperature changes, check that the sensor is properly connected and positioned. Also, make sure it’s not affected by drafts or direct sunlight. Radiator Not Heating Properly

If the radiator isn’t heating as expected, bleed the radiator to remove any trapped air. Also, check that the valve is fully open and that there are no blockages in the system. Leaks Around the Valve

For any leaks, first, try tightening the connections. If the problem persists, you may need to reapply thread sealant or replace damaged components.
