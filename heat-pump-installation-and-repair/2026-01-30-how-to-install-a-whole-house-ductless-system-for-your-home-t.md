We also explain how to install a whole-house ductless system below. How Ductless Systems Work

Ductless heat pumps transfer heat from one area to another. When you set them to cooling mode, they remove heat from inside your home and push it outside. You can reverse the process by switching to heating mode so that the same system extracts heat from the outdoor air and brings it inside. This heat transfer works because refrigerant circulates between the indoor and outdoor units.

Benefits of Whole-House Ductless Systems

Many homeowners are opting for whole-house ductless systems, also known as mini-split systems, because of their energy savings and flexibility. Here are some of their advantages:

Easy installation: Since the unit doesn’t have ductwork, installation is less invasive.

Energy efficiency: Ductless systems can be more efficient than traditional HVAC systems, so a homeowner may have lower energy bills.

Improved air quality: The system has built-in air filtration, which removes dust, allergens, and other particles from the air.

Quiet operation: Indoor units don’t make a lot of noise.

Zoned comfort: You can control each indoor unit separately, so you can customize the temperature in different rooms.

Planning Your Whole-House Ductless Installation

There are a few steps you can take to prepare for the installation process. Assessing Your Home’s Needs

The first step in planning your ductless system installation is to assess your home’s heating and cooling needs. This depends on the layout and size of your home, the number of rooms or zones you want to condition, and your local climate.

A professional HVAC technician can perform a heat load calculation to figure out your system’s correct sizing. This calculation accounts for several factors, including your home’s square footage, insulation levels, and window placement. Choosing the Right System Components

After your home assessment, you’ll have to choose the components for the ductless system, which includes the following:

A branch box to distribute refrigerant to multiple indoor units An outdoor condensing unit

Multiple indoor air handlers, such as wall-mounted, ceiling-mounted, or ducted Programmable thermostats for each zone

Refrigerant lines to connect the indoor and outdoor units

Your home’s layout and individual preferences will help you decide the number and type of indoor units you’ll need. Trethewey explains that you can connect a single outdoor unit to multiple indoor air handlers, which provides zoned comfort throughout the home.

On a recent This Old House project, the team found that a single outdoor condensing unit paired with branch boxes could replace what would have otherwise required five or six individual condensers lined up outside the building. By running two refrigerant lines from the outdoor unit into a branch box inside the building, each box can serve up to four zones—meaning two boxes off a single unit can handle up to eight zones throughout the home. This dramatically reduces outdoor equipment clutter and noise near the building.

Installation Process for a Ductless System

Installing a whole-house ductless system is a complex task, so unless you’re an avid DIYer with significant plumbing and electrical experience, we recommend you hire an HVAC technician to complete the project.

Whether you’re a homeowner with the right skill set or you plan on hiring a professional, understanding the process can help you make informed decisions and have a successful installation. Here’s how to install your whole-house ductless system. Outdoor Unit Installation

Place the outdoor condensing unit on a stable, level surface, which can be a concrete pad or mounted on brackets attached to the exterior wall. For the system to work properly in colder climates, you have to mount the unit above the snow line. Your contractor will factor in the following: Adequate airflow around the unit

Aesthetic considerations and impact on your home’s exterior appearance

Local building codes and zoning regulations Proximity to the indoor units

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey, This Old House plumbing and heating expert, explains the importance of placement in cold climates: “If we put it down on a concrete pad, like we normally do, we’d worry about snow. So we actually wanna hang it off the side of the building.” Wall-mounted brackets with lag screws keep the unit above the snow line and ensure reliable operation through winter.

Clearance matters more than you might think. On a This Old House project, the crew specified at least 12 inches between the condenser and the building, plus 12 inches of clearance on each side to ensure proper airflow. Homeowners may also need to clear nearby shrubbery to maintain those gaps. In tight urban environments where ground space is limited—think narrow sidewalks or adjacent parking—wall-mounting the unit above head height with a purpose-built bracket can be the best solution. Indoor Unit Installation

You can install indoor air handlers in various configurations according to your home’s layout and your preferences. Options include the following:

Ceiling-mounted units: These work in rooms with limited wall space.

Ducted units: You can hide ducted units in attics or crawl spaces to provide conditioned air through short duct runs.

Wall-mounted units: Homeowners choose these for open areas and individual rooms.

Each indoor unit requires a connection to the outdoor unit via refrigerant lines, a condensate drain line, and electrical wiring. Here’s how the HVAC technician will install your unit:

Choose the appropriate locations for each indoor unit.

Create small openings in walls or ceilings for refrigerant lines and wiring. Mount the indoor units securely.

Connect refrigerant lines, drain lines, and electrical connections.

Insulate and seal all penetrations to maintain energy efficiency.

The refrigerant lines connect the outdoor unit to the indoor units. This is how the system transfers heat. For multi-zone systems, your contractor will use a branch box to distribute refrigerant to multiple indoor units. Here’s what’s involved in this step:

Charging the system with the correct amount of refrigerant

Connecting smaller refrigerant lines from the branch box to each indoor unit

Ensuring all the refrigerant lines are properly insulated

Evacuating the lines to remove air and moisture

Running refrigerant lines from the outdoor unit to the branch box

Pressure testing the system to check for leaks

Electrical Connections and Thermostat Installation

Each indoor unit needs electrical connections and a thermostat for individual zone control. The installation process includes the following steps:

Connect power and control wiring between the outdoor and indoor units.

Configure the system for optimal performance and energy efficiency.

Install programmable thermostats for each zone.

Run electrical wiring from the main electrical panel to the outdoor unit.
